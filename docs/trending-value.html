How the list is built

Every stock above ₹500 Cr that trades at least ₹1 Cr a day is scored 1–10 on 6 valuation factors — P/E, P/B, P/CF, P/S, EV/EBITDA, Div Yield — where 1 is the cheapest decile of the market and 10 the most expensive. Add them up and you get the Value Composite (6 = cheap on everything, 60 = expensive on everything). Keep the cheapest 10% (composite ≤ 17), then sort those by 6-month price return and take the top 25.

The two-step matters: cheap-only screens buy declining businesses, and momentum-only screens buy whatever is already expensive. Requiring both is what O'Shaughnessy found beat either leg on its own.

What this page does not know

The evidence for Trending Value is US data from 1964–2009. Nothing here has validated it on Indian equities — not this repo, and not, to my knowledge, anywhere with comparable rigour. It is also a basket strategy rebalanced monthly: in the research the 25 names are held together and refreshed as a group, and individual positions failing is normal and expected. Reading the top row as a stock tip is not how the strategy works. Every pick is logged to the outcome ledger from today, so in a few months the scoreboard will have a local measurement instead of a borrowed one.

Method, factor coverage and caveats (tap to expand)

Why negative P/E scores 10 and not 1

Sorted numerically, a company at −4× earnings looks like the cheapest stock in the market. It is not cheap; it is loss-making. Negative earnings, negative book value, negative cash flow and negative EBITDA are therefore hard-assigned the worst decile and never ranked. This single rule is the difference between a value screen and a list of companies in trouble.

Missing data vs bad data

A factor with no data is dropped for that stock and the composite is renormalised over what remains — minimum 4 of 6 factors, else the stock is excluded entirely. Scoring a data gap as "expensive" would measure this repo's data coverage rather than the company's valuation. A non-payer of dividends is different: zero yield is a real observation, so it correctly scores 10.

Financials are scored differently

For banks, NBFCs and insurers, EV/EBITDA and P/CF describe nothing real — debt is raw material rather than leverage, and operating cash flow swings with the loan book. Both factors are dropped for financials and the composite renormalises over the rest. They are marked financial on the row so you can see which comparison you are making.

Why the decile is cut by count, not by score

Six integer deciles can only produce 55 distinct composites, so on a 1,532-stock universe roughly 28 names share every score. Keeping everything at or below a threshold would then return about 20% of the market and call it a decile. So the cut takes the cheapest 10% by count, and where composites tie it prefers the name with the better average percentile rank across the factors — the same ordering at full resolution. O'Shaughnessy sums 1–100 percentile ranks for exactly this reason; the 1–10 scale is kept here because it is readable, with the finer ranking used only to break ties.

Why the list is capped at 3 per industry

The first live run of this screen returned seven sugar mills in 25 names, plus ten in Materials — a "diversified basket" that was really one bet on the sugar cycle placed seven times. That is not a bug in the composite; it is what a mechanical cheap-decile screen does when a whole industry gets cheap together.

O'Shaughnessy's published rules are silent on sector limits, because the US universe he tested is large enough that clustering is rarer. On roughly 1,600 tradeable Indian names it is the normal case, so the list is capped at 3 per industry, applied in momentum order. Grouping is by industry rather than sector deliberately: the data source files four sugar mills under Consumer Staples and two under Materials, so a sector cap would still have admitted six of them.

Where a cap slot cannot be filled without breaching it, the name is admitted and marked over cap rather than shipping a list of 14 — and the mix above always shows what the uncapped list would have looked like.

Value traps and the trap-risk flag

Momentum is meant to be the trap filter: a genuinely broken business rarely has a strong 6-month return. It is an imperfect filter. Rows where ROCE is under 10% or ROE under 8% carry a trap risk mark — cheap and earning poorly on capital is the classic pattern. These are not excluded, because excluding them would no longer be the tested strategy, but you should know which ones they are.

What was excluded and why

From the full market: 3,845 below ₹500 Cr, 469 below ₹1 Cr average daily traded value (an unknown ADV counts as untradeable — the screen fails closed), 0 under NSE surveillance, 32 with fewer than 4 usable factors. Surveillance list is 0h old.

How this differs from Creamy Layer and Multibagger

Creamy Layer and Multibagger hunt quality and growth, and mostly surface businesses that are expensive for good reasons. This page does the opposite: it ranks on cheapness first and only asks about price behaviour second. Overlap between them is rare and worth a second look when it happens.
